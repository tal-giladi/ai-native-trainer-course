# Instructor notes — 18.2 Evidence-based technical writing

**Teaching objective.** Students write public pieces whose every claim sits at rung 3 or below on the claims ladder, with the source (and for experimental effects, the interval) in the same sentence, a limits line, a disclosure line and no hype, and they can explain why this is a professional and legal floor, not a stylistic preference. Their first piece passes `PostCheck claims` with their own evidence files and private deny list.

**Likely confusion.** Why the source has to be in the *same* sentence rather than in a footnote or at the end. Answer: sentences get quoted and screenshotted on their own; the one that travels must carry its source. Second: students ask whether evidence IDs like `EXP-01` mean anything to a stranger. They do if the method notes are public or the piece links the report; otherwise use the link.

**Common misconception.** "Honesty means hedging." Students swing from "10x" to "might possibly help some teams in some cases". Both fail the reader. The fix is a plain claim at its rung plus a separate limits line. Second: "Hype words are harmless style." Show the Sumner result and the FTC "studies show" rule: they are how overclaims start and how they become liabilities.

**Key analogy.** A pull request description. A good one says what changed, links the ticket and the test run, and names what was not tested. "Massively improved performance!!!" gets rejected in review; so should the post.

**Common failure in the exercise.** Running `claims` without `--evidence`, so unknown IDs pass. Second: keeping the employer's name because "it's public that I work there"; the deny list exists because clients and employers have not agreed to be in your marketing (14.4). Third: fixing errors by deleting numbers until the piece says nothing.

**Expected exercise outcome.** One published piece with the header filled in, passing `claims` and `format`, restated by a colleague without inflation. For the break: at least nine of the eleven errors named with the rule they break, and a rewrite close to the reference that keeps one effect claim with its interval.

**Extension exercise.** Take a popular post about AI coding productivity from your feed. Put each claim on the ladder, find the primary source for each number, and write the three-sentence version the evidence supports.

**Discussion question.** A client's marketing team wants to quote your workshop's normalized gain of 0.57 as "57% better developers". What do you say, and what would you agree to let them publish?
