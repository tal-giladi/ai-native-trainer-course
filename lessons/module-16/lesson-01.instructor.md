# Instructor notes — 16.1 How adults and engineers learn

**Teaching objective.** Students stop using enjoyment as a proxy for learning. They can place any training number on Kirkpatrick's four levels, explain the fluency illusion from working memory and schemas, classify activities with ICAP, and write an observable learning claim for one concept from their own method, grounded in three real conversations.

**Likely confusion.** "If they enjoyed it, surely they learned something?" Sometimes, but the correlation is weak and can even run backwards (Deslauriers). The second confusion: students hear "active learning" as "more energy" or "more questions to the room". Asking "any questions?" is not active; making every learner decide something is.

**Common misconception.** "Engineers learn best by watching an expert, then trying it on their own later." The watching is fine as a start; the "later" rarely happens, and nothing in the session checks it. Second: "adults are self-directed, so structure is patronizing." Adults new to a concept need structure on that concept (see expertise reversal in 16.2); what they resent is structure on what they already know.

**Key analogy.** Watching someone play a video game well versus holding the controller. After an hour of watching you can describe the level; you cannot finish it. The feedback form asks how the stream was.

**Common failure in the exercise.** Prior-knowledge chats turn into pitches ("have you heard of shadow rules?"). Remind students of 01.4: ask about the last time something happened, never about your concept by name. Second: learning claims that describe the session ("participants will see a live demo of…") rather than the learner.

**Expected exercise outcome.** A chosen concept with status *supported*; three chats with at least two misconceptions quoted verbatim; a one-sentence learning claim with a role, a situation and an observable performance; an instinctive five-line sketch labelled P/A/C/I, typically 70–100% passive. For the break: the student names the fluency illusion, the per-objective gains, the I1-only improvement and the 21 passive minutes, without blaming the audience.

**Extension exercise.** Take a conference talk you enjoyed in the last year. Without rewatching, write down three things you can now *do* because of it. Then rewatch the first ten minutes. What fraction of the content survived, and what kind (story, name, procedure)?

**Discussion question.** A client says: "We only care that people like the training; if they like it they will use it." What would you measure in the first delivery to find out whether they are right, and what would you say if the numbers showed they were?
