# Instructor notes — 16.4 Managing the room

**Teaching objective.** Students prepare the room as carefully as the content: a question triage they can apply in seconds, write-first prompts and real wait time, entry points and extensions for mixed levels, role-based pairs, three rehearsed objection cards built on their concept's evidence and boundary, and remote mechanics (co-host, pre-pasted breakouts, chat checks).

**Likely confusion.** "Managing the room means keeping control." It means keeping the room on the objectives while making it safe to be wrong. Second: students think a skeptic must be won over during the session. The goal is a fair, short, evidence-based answer and an offer to test; winning is optional.

**Common misconception.** "Experts in the room should help by answering questions." Uncontrolled, they answer everything and the novices stop thinking. Give experts the explaining role in pairs and the extension task, not the microphone. Second: "remote is in-person with a camera." Remote removes almost all of your signals; it needs a co-host and more frequent checks, not more slides.

**Key analogy.** Incident command. The plan is the runbook; the room is the incident. You triage, you delegate (co-host, pairs), you communicate on a schedule, you keep a log (the parking lot), and you follow up afterwards. You do not debug the loudest alert for four minutes while the rest of the system burns.

**Common failure in the exercise.** Objection cards that concede nothing ("Concede: the question shows interest"). Push for a specific true statement. Second: evidence lines with no number where the concept card has one. Third: skeptic rehearsal with a friendly colleague who plays soft; give the colleague the three hardest objections from the Module 17 bank if they run dry.

**Expected exercise outcome.** A room plan with entry points, extensions, a pairing rule and remote logistics; three objection cards with specific concessions, evidence with intervals, boundaries and offers; every "any questions?" replaced; a recorded rehearsal with answers under 90 seconds. For the break: all eight incidents mapped to a protocol with a one-sentence better move.

**Extension exercise.** Run the same five-minute segment of your session twice with a friend, once in person and once over a call, with a second friend watching both. Ask the observer to count the signals you responded to in each. What did you miss remotely, and which tool would have caught it?

**Discussion question.** A client's CTO sits in on your workshop and, ten minutes in, says loudly that agents are a fad. Everyone else in the room reports to them. How does the skeptic protocol change, and what do you do in the follow-up?
