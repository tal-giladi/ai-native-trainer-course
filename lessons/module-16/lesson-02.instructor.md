# Instructor notes — 16.2 Objectives, alignment and cognitive load

**Teaching objective.** Students design backwards: claim → two or three observable objectives with levels → items at those levels on two parallel forms → activities that practise the same verbs. They cut extraneous load, sequence worked → faded → full → transfer, and build at least one misconception item from real learners' words. `LearnCheck align` passes on their plan.

**Likely confusion.** "Isn't the level column bureaucracy?" Show that the level is the only thing that tells you whether an item tests the objective. Second: students conflate intrinsic and extraneous load and try to "simplify the concept" instead of simplifying the presentation. The concept stays; the nine terms, three windows and irrelevant code go.

**Common misconception.** "Good engineers don't need worked examples." On their own stack, true (expertise reversal). On a concept that is new to them, a worked example is still the fastest route. Second: "multiple choice can only test recall." Diff-based items test analysis; the distractors do the work.

**Key analogy.** Requirements, tests and implementation. An objective is a requirement; an item is its test; an activity is the implementation. A requirement with no test, a test for a requirement nobody wrote, and an implementation for neither are all bugs, and backward design is test-driven development for teaching.

**Common failure in the exercise.** Items that are all at "remember" because they are quick to write. Ask "what does the learner *do* to answer this?" Second: parallel forms that are identical except for a variable name, so learners recognize them; change the business term and the file, keep the structure. Third: rubrics written after seeing the answers.

**Expected exercise outcome.** Two or three objectives with condition, verb, criterion and level; four to eight items per form with rubrics; at least one non-example and two distractors quoted from chats; a load list with at least a third of the terms cut. `align` clean in the objectives section (the time section may still fail until 16.3). For the break: all twelve errors explained and a rewrite that merges O1/O2, drops O4, and replaces recall items.

**Extension exercise.** Take the Module 14 paraphrase test (14.2). Rewrite it as two items at the right level for the objective "a non-engineer can explain the loop". What does the paraphrase test do that a multiple-choice item cannot, and vice versa?

**Discussion question.** A client asks for a workshop whose objective is "developers trust the coding agent more". Is that an objective in this lesson's sense? What would you write instead, and what would you measure to see whether trust changed for good reasons?
