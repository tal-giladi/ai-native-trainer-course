# Instructor notes — 17.3 The hard-questions bank

**Teaching objective.** Students collect real questions from their FAQ, discovery calls and parking lots, cover the five categories every paid room raises, and write one-minute cards in the shape concede, evidence, boundary, not known, bridge, with every number checked against its source and placed on the claims ladder. They say the cards aloud to unseen questions and triage them between wait-time and the Q&A block.

**Likely confusion.** "The bank is a script." It is a set of shapes and evidence; students who memorize wording freeze when the question comes in different words, which it always does. Second confusion: "not known" as weakness. It is the sentence that makes the rest believable, and experienced audiences listen for it.

**Common misconception.** "Confidence wins the room." Confident overclaiming wins the enthusiasts and loses the people who decide on a second engagement: the staff engineer, the security lead, the manager who has to defend the budget. Second: "a citation makes a number safe." Only if the source says that number for that kind of work.

**Key analogy.** An expert witness under cross-examination. The witness who concedes what is true, states what the evidence shows and where it stops, and says "I don't know" when they don't, is the one the jury believes. The witness who claims certainty is the one the other side takes apart.

**Common failure in the exercise.** Cards with evidence that is a vague source ("studies show", "my experience"). Answers that restate the question and then tell a story. Security answers that rely on the model refusing. Confidentiality answers that deflect. Numbers borrowed from headlines rather than the paper.

**Expected exercise outcome.** A bank of at least twelve cards covering the five categories, 0 errors from `KitCheck questions`, every number with its ladder rung noted, and a recorded run with unseen questions in which most answers keep the shape and stay under about 75 seconds. For the break: the two hype errors, the two missing-evidence errors, the missing confidentiality category, the 166-word answer, and the misattributed 55% that the checker cannot catch.

**Extension exercise.** Run a "red-team Q&A": two colleagues get the question list and the brief to be difficult, one friendly-hostile (a skeptic with a real case), one derailing (off-topic, long). Score each answer on the observation rubric's Questions and Claims criteria (17.4).

**Discussion question.** A host asks you not to mention the METR slowdown result because "it will confuse the team". What do you do if a participant raises it, and should you raise it yourself?
