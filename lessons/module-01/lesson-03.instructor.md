# Instructor notes — 01.3 Finding your wedge

**Teaching objective.** Students write a one-sentence wedge (stack × audience × pain) with an explicit "out" list, score three candidates on five weighted criteria with evidence, count reachable people honestly, and name the 3–4 platforms they will build against for the rest of the course.

**Likely confusion.** Stack versus tool. Students put "Claude Code" or "Copilot" in the stack and leave out the database. Draw the line: the stack is where the team's work lives (language, database, tracker, docs, code host, CI); agents are what the team currently licenses, and they go in the inventory, not in the wedge sentence.

**Common misconception.** "Narrow means fewer clients." In practice narrow means being remembered and believed. The trainer who says "legacy .NET and SQL Server" is the first name a .NET CTO recalls; the one who says "AI for teams" is not recalled at all. Second misconception: the wedge is permanent. It is a hypothesis that 01.4 is designed to break.

**Key analogy.** A specialist doctor. A general "health consultant" competes with everyone; a hand surgeon gets every hand referral in the region. The specialization is where trust comes from, not where the market ends.

**Common failure in the exercise.** Scores with no evidence line, or evidence written after the score to justify it. Make them write the evidence first. Second: the funnel built from survey percentages multiplied together instead of a search of their own contacts. Third: no "out" list, which always means the wedge is not narrow yet.

**Expected exercise outcome.** Three scored candidates with totals typically spread from about 2.5 to 4.3; a chosen wedge whose pain score is 3–4 (a hypothesis) rather than 5; a funnel of 15–40 reachable people with counted and assumed steps marked; a stack inventory with several `?` cells that become interview questions.

**Extension exercise.** Find three job ads from companies inside your wedge. Fill a second column of your stack inventory from them alone. Where do the job ads disagree with your assumptions?

**Discussion question.** Your strongest advantage might be your current employer's stack. Where does "my wedge is what I know from work" become a conflict with your employer, and what would you check before your first external conversation?
