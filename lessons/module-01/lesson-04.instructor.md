# Instructor notes — 01.4 Customer discovery interviews

**Teaching objective.** Students critique a leading-question transcript by hand and with a heuristic linter, rewrite defective questions as past-specific ones, run 3–5 consented real interviews with verbatim quotes and a commitment ask, build a pain map from past events only, and reach a keep / change / kill verdict on their wedge.

**Likely confusion.** Open versus past-specific. "What are your biggest challenges with AI?" is open but generic; it gets opinions. The target is not open questions but questions about a specific past event. Second: students think a commitment means a purchase. In discovery it means the participant spends something now: an intro, data, a second session.

**Common misconception.** "I'll just ask them if they'd buy it — that's the real test." Future promises are the least reliable evidence there is, and asking about price turns research into sales before there is anything to sell. A second misconception: that engineers are immune to leading questions because they are analytical. Loftus and Palmer's participants were not unintelligent either; the effect is about language, not intelligence.

**Key analogy.** A detective interviewing a witness. A good detective never asks "Was the car red?"; they ask "What do you remember about the car?" and then "What happened next?". Evidence that the interviewer supplied is inadmissible.

**Common failure in the exercise.** Hand critiques that catch leading and hypothetical questions but miss the dropped threads (Q5, Q7, Q15), which are the costliest defects. Ask them, for every past-event answer, "what was the next question, and was it about that event?". In real interviews: students pitch in the last five minutes "just to get feedback", which retroactively colors the notes. Also: notes that paraphrase instead of quoting; insist on quotation marks.

**Expected exercise outcome.** A critique finding defects in 15–17 of 18 questions, the three evidence threads, and two linter misses (Q9, Q17) plus "cannot see missed threads". Three to five interview notes, most answers on rungs 4–7, at least one commitment given. A pain map with one or two pains heard in two or more independent interviews, often *not* the pain the student hypothesized. A verdict of "change" is the most common and the healthiest outcome.

**Extension exercise.** Deliberately interview one person you expect to disagree with your wedge (someone who thinks agents are overhyped, or whose team banned them). What did they say that your other interviews did not? Add it to the pain map or to the parking lot, and explain your choice.

**Discussion question.** Your best evidence came from a participant who happened to volunteer an incident despite a bad question. How many interviews in your own career — hiring, requirements, incident reviews — do you think were decided by what the interviewer put into the question?
