# Instructor notes — 20.1 Positioning and the offer ladder

**Teaching objective.** Students write a positioning sentence in the buyer's words and a three-to-five-rung ladder in which each rung produces the evidence for the next, with fixed prices, entry criteria and next steps, and remove invented scarcity, decoys and guarantees. `OfferCheck ladder` clean.

**Likely confusion.** Positioning vs the method's name. Students want to lead with "Ground-Bound-Build-Prove" because they built it. Buyers do not buy a method; they hire you for a job. The method appears in the "I [what you do]" slot, at most. Second: whether a free discovery call is a rung. It is not; it is part of selling, and putting it on the ladder invites people who only want free advice.

**Common misconception.** "Scarcity and urgency are just marketing; everyone does it." Some do, and business buyers discount it, but a claim you invented is still a false statement to someone you want to trust you. A stated cap you keep is both honest and useful. Second: "A guarantee shows confidence." It shows you are willing to promise what you cannot measure; experienced buyers read it as naivety or as a sign the contract will be fought over.

**Key analogy.** Staged rollout with feature flags. You would not ship a change to all users at once; you ship to one cohort, measure, and widen only if the guardrails hold. The ladder is a staged rollout of the client's trust and money, with the pilot as the canary and a pre-registered rollback rule.

**Common failure in the exercise.** Ladders with a single real rung (the workshop) and three aspirational ones with no evidence. Push students to label unsupported rungs "after N pilots" or leave them off. Second: prices copied from someone else's website without a worksheet; accept placeholders now, but say that 20.2 must replace them. Third: positioning sentences over 60 words; one audience, one problem.

**Expected exercise outcome.** `workshop-kit/offer-ladder.md` with a five-slot positioning sentence, a capacity line, 3–5 rungs each with evidence IDs (at least EXP-01-style for the pilot), entry criteria and next steps; a "what is not on the ladder" list; `ladder` clean.

**Extension exercise.** Show the ladder to two people from your wedge audience without explaining it. Ask each: "Which rung would you buy first, and what would you need to see before the next one?" Rewrite any rung whose next-step evidence they describe differently from you.

**Discussion question.** A prospect says: "We don't want a pilot; we want the full implementation now, and we'll pay more for speed." Do you skip the pilot rung? What do you lose, and what could you put in the implementation SOW so that the first weeks do the pilot's job?
