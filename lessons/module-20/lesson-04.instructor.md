# Instructor notes — 20.4 Proposals, SOWs and scope control

**Teaching objective.** Students write a proposal in the client's words with priced options, evidence IDs and a "not promised" section; a SOW with results-based deliverables and checkable acceptance criteria, exclusions, responsibilities, change control, a measurement section instead of a guarantee and fees that add up; and a change log that turns every scope request into a recorded decision. All three `OfferCheck` commands clean.

**Likely confusion.** Proposal vs SOW. The proposal persuades and offers options; the SOW binds one option. Students often write a SOW-like proposal (too long, no client words) or a proposal-like SOW (benefits, adjectives). Second: acceptance criteria vs outcomes. The report deliverable is accepted when it honestly states the pre-registered outcomes, whatever they are; the outcomes are not the acceptance criterion.

**Common misconception.** "A generous scope wins clients." Vague scope wins arguments later. Clients value knowing exactly what they get. Second: "Refusing a guarantee looks weak." In practice it is the most credible thing in the proposal, especially next to a measurement section and a probability of loss. Third: "Free extras don't need logging." They create expectations; a logged concession is a gift, an unlogged one a precedent.

**Key analogy.** An API contract. The SOW is the interface: inputs (client responsibilities, access), outputs (deliverables with acceptance tests), error cases (stop rule, termination), and versioning (change control, SOW version). Silent scope creep is an undocumented breaking change.

**Common failure in the exercise.** Acceptance criteria that restate the deliverable ("Workshop — workshop delivered"). Exclusions that list only unlikely things; ask "what will they assume is included?". Payment schedules edited without updating the total. Proposals with three options where one is a decoy (20.1). Forgetting procurement time in the schedule.

**Expected exercise outcome.** A proposal sent to the real pitch target by the promised date, `proposal` clean; a draft SOW with `sow` clean and sections 12–14 flagged for legal review; an empty `change-log.md` ready from day one.

**Extension exercise.** Swap SOWs with another student and play the client's procurement officer: find three sentences you could read in the client's favour and one scope request the SOW does not answer. The author rewrites and reruns `sow`.

**Discussion question.** The client accepts the proposal but wants to pay on "net 90" and use their own contract, which assigns "all IP created or used" to them. Which do you negotiate, which do you accept, and which is a walk-away point for you?
